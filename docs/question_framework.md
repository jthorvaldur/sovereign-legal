# The Question Framework: Ask-King Methodology

> "The one who asks the questions controls the conversation. The one who answers the questions accepts the premises."

In law, silence is consent. A statement is a position — it can be attacked, rebutted, and used against you. A question is a quest — it seeks, it challenges, it demands disclosure. The person asking questions maintains sovereignty. The person answering them submits to the questioner's frame.

This is not a rhetorical trick. It is a jurisdictional strategy. Every question challenges the court to prove its authority. Every statement accepts the court's authority as given.

---

## Why Questions Work

### 1. Questions Force Disclosure

A court that cannot answer "Under what authority do you operate?" has revealed something critical: it either does not know, or it does not want you to know. Both are actionable.

When you ask a question, the court must either:
- **Answer truthfully** — which may reveal the limits of its jurisdiction
- **Refuse to answer** — which creates a record of non-disclosure
- **Evade** — which can be noted on the record as evasion
- **Sanction you for asking** — which reveals that the court punishes inquiry, itself a due process issue

Every outcome is strategically useful.

### 2. Questions Break the Consent Loop

The court system runs on consent. Filing a petition = consent. Responding to a motion = consent. Accepting the court's vocabulary = consent. Answering the court's questions = consent.

Questions interrupt this loop. Instead of accepting the court's frame ("You are the Respondent, here is your obligation"), you challenge the frame ("By what authority am I designated Respondent? Where is the contract that binds me to this designation?").

The court cannot proceed without either answering the question (revealing its jurisdictional basis) or ignoring it (creating a due process record).

### 3. Questions Preserve Standing

In every jurisdiction, the party that asks questions maintains a higher standing than the party that answers them. This is not a cultural convention — it is a structural feature of legal proceedings:

- The **questioner** defines the scope of inquiry
- The **answerer** is limited to the scope defined by the questioner
- The **questioner** controls the pace, direction, and depth of the exchange
- The **answerer** reacts to the questioner's agenda

By defaulting to questions, you maintain the position of the sovereign — the one who inquires — rather than the subject — the one who is examined.

### 4. Etymology Reveals the Strategy

**Question** — from Latin *quaestionem*, "a seeking, inquiry." Contains "quest" — a purposeful journey of discovery. The questioner is the seeker, the active party, the one on a mission.

**Answer** — from Old English *andswaru*, "a sworn response." The answerer is SWORN — bound by their response. An answer is an obligation. A question is a freedom.

**Ask** — in many traditions, "ask" and "king" share a root. The one who asks IS the sovereign. The king does not answer to the court — the court answers to the king.

## The Pattern Library

### Challenging Jurisdiction

These questions force the court to identify and justify its jurisdictional basis.

| Context | Question |
|---------|----------|
| Opening | "Under what specific authority — natural law, common law, statutory, or commercial — does this court claim jurisdiction over this matter?" |
| Jurisdiction | "Has jurisdiction been established by consent of the parties, by statute, or by some other basis? If by consent, when was that consent given and was it informed?" |
| Authority | "Is this court a court of record operating under the common law, or a court of equity operating under legislative authority?" |
| Jury | "Does this court recognize my right to a trial by jury? If not, under what jurisdiction has that right been waived, and when did I consent to that waiver?" |
| Identity | "Is this court addressing me as a living man/woman, or as the corporate fiction designated by the ALL CAPS name on the filing?" |
| Scope | "What are the outer limits of this court's jurisdiction? What matters does it acknowledge are BEYOND its authority?" |

### Demanding Disclosure

These questions require the court to reveal information it may prefer to conceal.

| Context | Question |
|---------|----------|
| Court type | "For the record, is this proceeding governed by common law, equity, statute, or commercial/admiralty law?" |
| Authority basis | "What is the source document that establishes this court's authority over the subject matter?" |
| Consent | "Can the court identify the specific act or document by which I consented to its jurisdiction?" |
| Rights | "Does this court acknowledge that rights exist which are beyond its authority to grant or revoke?" |
| Financial | "Is this court operating in its capacity as a commercial venue settling financial disputes between legal entities?" |
| Oath | "Has the presiding judge taken an oath to uphold the Constitution? If so, does that oath include the protection of rights that preexist the Constitution?" |

### Asserting Standing

These questions establish your position without making statements that could be used against you.

| Context | Question |
|---------|----------|
| Identity | "Am I not a living man/woman, standing here in my own capacity, not as a representative of any corporate entity?" |
| Rights | "Are my natural rights as a parent not superior to any statutory designation this court may impose?" |
| Bond | "Is the bond between a mother and her offspring not a matter of natural law, beyond the jurisdiction of any court of equity?" |
| Consent | "Have I not reserved all rights, waiving none, and consented to nothing that has not been fully disclosed?" |
| Authority | "Does any man or woman in this room claim personal authority over my offspring? Not institutional authority — personal, individual authority?" |
| Standing | "Do I not have standing to challenge the jurisdiction of this court before submitting to its procedures?" |

### Responding to Court Questions

When the court asks you questions, respond with questions. Never accept the court's premises.

| Court asks | You respond |
|-----------|-------------|
| "How do you plead?" | "To what specific charge, under what jurisdiction, and by what authority is a plea demanded?" |
| "Do you understand?" | "Do you mean 'understand' as in comprehend, or 'stand under' as in submit to your authority? I comprehend English. I do not consent to standing under any authority that has not established its jurisdiction." |
| "State your name." | "Which name does this court require — the name of the living man, or the name of the corporate fiction on your documents?" |
| "Will you comply with this order?" | "Is this an order of a court of competent jurisdiction? Has jurisdiction been established? On what basis?" |
| "Do you have counsel?" | "Am I not competent to speak for myself? Does this court presume that a living man requires permission to address a tribunal?" |

## Rules of Engagement

### 1. Never Answer a Question with a Statement

**Wrong:** "I don't recognize this court's authority."
(This is a statement. It can be argued, rebutted, and sanctioned.)

**Right:** "Has this court established its jurisdiction over me? By what specific mechanism?"
(This is a question. It demands a response. It puts the burden on the court.)

### 2. Never Accept Premises

**Wrong:** Answering "How much child support should you pay?" with "I think $500 is fair."
(By answering, you have accepted that: you owe child support, the court has authority to set the amount, and you are the PERSON designated in the filing.)

**Right:** "Before we discuss amounts, has it been established under what jurisdiction this obligation arises? Is this a common law debt, a statutory obligation, or a commercial settlement? And is it owed by the living man or by the corporate entity?"

### 3. Always Put Questions on the Record

Questions asked in open court, on the record, create a permanent trail. If the court refuses to answer, that refusal is on the record. If the court answers, the answer is on the record. Either way, the record preserves the challenge.

"For the record, I am asking this court to identify its jurisdictional basis before proceeding."

### 4. Stack Questions

Don't ask one question and wait. Stack them so the court must address a complete framework:

"For the record: Under what jurisdiction does this court operate? By what specific mechanism did I consent to that jurisdiction? Does this court acknowledge rights that exist beyond its authority? And is the party before this court a living man, or a corporate fiction?"

Each unanswered question compounds the jurisdictional challenge.

### 5. Never Show Anger — Show Curiosity

Anger is a statement. It accepts the court's frame and fights within it.

Curiosity is a question. It steps outside the court's frame and examines it.

"I'm not challenging this court out of anger. I am simply seeking to understand the basis of its authority, as is my right."

---

## The Strategic Logic

Every legal system, at its core, is a game with rules. The rules are defined by the jurisdiction. If you play within the jurisdiction, you play by its rules.

**Questions step OUTSIDE the rules and examine them.** The court cannot punish you for asking about its own authority — or if it does, that punishment itself becomes evidence of jurisdictional overreach.

The goal is not to "win" within the court's system. The goal is to force the court to reveal WHICH system it is using — because once the system is visible, its limits are visible. And its limits are where your rights begin.
